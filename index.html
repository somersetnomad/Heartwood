Canopy +18 to +30 m

Where light becomes tree.

A large oak spreads perhaps a quarter of a million leaves to the sun. Each one is a thin green factory, open to the air through hundreds of microscopic pores in every square millimetre of its underside.

Under the microscope · a leaf in cross-section

Inside one leaf, at four hundred times

Light falls through the clear skin of the leaf into tall palisade cells, each packed with chloroplasts. Watch them circle the cell walls: living cells stream their contents constantly, and the chloroplasts ride the current, turning to meet the light.

Below, loose spongy cells leave air spaces open to a single pore, the stoma. Carbon dioxide drifts in through it. Oxygen and water vapour drift out. Two guard cells swell and relax to open and close it.

Almost all of a tree was once air

The sugar a leaf makes is the raw material for everything else: cellulose for new wood, energy for roots, and a share sent underground to feed fungal partners. Very little of a tree's dry weight came from the soil. Nearly all of it is carbon pulled from the sky.

When soil dries, a tree closes its stomata to save water, and photosynthesis slows. A forest's breathing follows the weather.

Life in the crown · birds

A wood is built in storeys.

From the woodland floor to the top of the canopy, each layer of a tree is a different place to live. Birds divide it between them: some glean the outer twigs, some climb the bark, some need a hole that took a century to form.

5,000–20,000

Clark's nutcracker

In the mountains of western North America, a single nutcracker makes thousands of separate seed caches every autumn and remembers where most of them are. The ones it forgets grow. Whitebark pine cones do not open by themselves, so the tree depends on this bird to plant its seeds.

1,274

Fig trees and hornbills

A global review counted 1,274 species of birds and mammals known to eat figs. Because fig trees fruit at different times through the year, they feed animals when little else is ripe. Hornbills swallow the fruit and carry the seeds far across the forest.

30–40 m

Harpy eagle

One of the largest eagles on Earth nests in the crowns of the tallest rainforest trees, often kapok or Brazil nut, building great platforms of sticks, often 30 to 40 metres above the ground. Where the giant emergent trees are felled, the eagles cannot breed.

Messages on the wind

Trees are always saying something.

Not in words. In scent, colour, chemistry, electricity and even sound. Some messages are meant for other trees, some for animals, and some are simply overheard. Choose a message and watch who receives it.

Rivers in the sky

Forests make their own rain.

Every leaf loses water through its stomata as it takes in carbon dioxide. Across a whole forest this adds up to a river flowing upward. A single large Amazon tree can release around 1,000 litres of water into the air in a day.

The Amazon as a whole is estimated to send about 20 billion tonnes of water into the atmosphere each day, more than the 17 billion tonnes the Amazon River pours into the Atlantic. Winds carry this "flying river" inland, where it falls as rain, is breathed out again, and falls again further on. Rain that began over the Atlantic can be recycled by trees several times before it reaches the Andes, and some of it waters farms far to the south.

Trees seed clouds too. Pines, oaks and eucalyptus give off scented compounds such as isoprene and terpenes. In sunlight these react to form tiny particles that water vapour can condense on. The blue haze over the Blue Ridge and Great Smoky Mountains is partly this: the forest's own breath, made visible.

How strongly forests pull moist air inland is still argued. One idea, the "biotic pump", proposes that condensation over forests actively draws ocean air towards them. It is intriguing and contested. That forests recycle rainfall, and that clearing them dries regions downwind, is well supported.

      Limb one · Species

      Eight lives, eight ways of being a tree

      Botanists estimate there are around 73,000 tree species on Earth, and that roughly 9,000 of them have not yet been described. Each plate below is drawn fresh, branch by branch, as it comes into view.

      Limb two · Forest ecosystems

      A forest is more than its trees

      There are about three trillion trees on Earth, around 370 for every person alive. Together they make forests that behave like single living systems, with their own weather, soils and populations.

      27% OF FOREST AREA · FAR NORTH

      Boreal

      Spruce, pine, larch and birch in a ring around the top of the world. Cold slows decay, so vast stores of carbon build up in the soils and peat beneath them.

      16% · MID-LATITUDES

      Temperate

      Oak, beech, maple, lime. Seasons rule here: leaves out in spring, shed in autumn, a nutrient-rich litter layer feeding the soil every year.

      45% · NEAR THE EQUATOR

      Tropical

      Home to a large share of all land species. The Amazon recycles its own rain: water breathed out by trees falls again further inland.

      ON TROPICAL COASTS

      Mangrove

      Trees that stand in salt water on stilt roots. They break storm waves, shelter young fish, and can store several times more carbon per hectare than many forests on dry land.

      Forest-area shares are from the UN FAO's Global Forest Resources Assessment 2020; subtropical forests make up the remaining 11%.

      Limb three · A whole world on one tree

      2,300 species live with Britain's oaks

      A study of Britain's two native oaks listed 2,300 species of birds, mammals, insects and other invertebrates, lichens, mosses and fungi that use them. 326 of those are found only on oak. Lose the oaks and they go too. Tap a part of the tree to meet some of them.

      Limb four · Trees and climate

      Wood is stored air

      About half the dry weight of wood is carbon, and every tonne of that carbon was once 3.67 tonnes of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. Across the world, forests take up roughly 15.6 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide a year. Clearing and damaging forests releases about 8.1 billion tonnes, leaving a net uptake of around 7.6 billion tonnes.

      Trees also cool the land directly. A canopy shades the ground, and the water a tree breathes out takes heat with it as it evaporates. Tree-lined streets can be several degrees cooler on a summer afternoon than bare ones nearby.

      Planting trees helps, but it can't replace cutting fossil fuel emissions. Protecting old forests keeps carbon that took centuries to gather, and native, mixed woodland stores more and supports more life than single-species plantations. Under drought and heat, some forests are already taking up less carbon than they did, and parts of the southeastern Amazon have become a net source.

      How much air is in a tree?

      A mature broadleaf tree can hold a few tonnes of dry wood above and below ground. Old giants hold far more.

      Limb five · How trees speak to us

      What trees do to a human body

      We evolved among trees, and our bodies still respond to them. Some of the evidence is strong, some is early, and some is only a correlation. Here it is, sorted honestly.

      Controlled comparison1984

      A window onto trees

      Roger Ulrich compared patients recovering from the same gallbladder surgery in one Pennsylvania hospital. Those whose window looked out on trees went home sooner, needed fewer strong painkillers, and drew fewer negative comments in their nurses' notes than matched patients facing a brick wall.

      Large correlation10 trees

      Street trees in Toronto

      Comparing health surveys with a map of 530,000 street trees, researchers found that ten more trees on a city block was linked to people rating their own health as highly as people seven years younger, or $10,000 richer. It shows a link, not proof that the trees caused it.

      Natural experiment100 million

      When the ash trees died

      The emerald ash borer killed about 100 million ash trees across the eastern United States. In the 15 states studied, counties hit by the beetle saw about 15,000 more deaths from heart disease and 6,000 more from lower respiratory disease than expected. An association, but a striking one.

      Small trials1982

      Forest bathing

      Japan's forestry agency coined shinrin-yoku, forest bathing: simply being among trees, slowly, with all your senses. Short trials have found lower stress hormones, pulse and blood pressure after forest walks compared with city walks. Many studies are small, so the size of the benefit is still uncertain.

      The senses trees speak to

      Smell. Pine and cedar woods smell the way they do because of terpenes like pinene. The scent after rain, petrichor, comes partly from oils plants release in dry weather.

      Sound. English once had a word for the sound of wind in the trees: psithurism. Each species sounds different. Aspen leaves on flat stalks shiver at the faintest breeze; pines hiss; oaks roar.

      Temperature. Under a canopy on a hot day the air is cooler and moister, partly from shade and partly because the leaves are evaporating water like sweat.

      An honest balance: tree pollen causes hay fever for many people, and some trees, like oaks and poplars, release isoprene, which can add to ozone smog where traffic pollution is heavy. Choosing the right tree for a street matters.

      Limb six · Trees in human culture

      The oldest companions we have

      Many cultures have imagined the world itself as a tree: roots in the underworld, trunk through the living world, crown in the heavens. It is the same shape this page is built on.

      1. c. 500 BCE

        The Bodhi tree

        Buddhist tradition holds that Siddhartha Gautama reached enlightenment beneath a sacred fig at Bodh Gaya. A cutting from that line, planted at Anuradhapura in Sri Lanka in 288 BCE, still grows. It is often called the oldest human-planted tree with a known planting date.

      2. c. 400 CE

        Ogham

        An early Irish alphabet carved on standing stones. Medieval scholars later linked its letters to trees, birch for the first, rowan for the second. The tree names may be a later layer of meaning, but they have shaped how the script is remembered.

      3. 1200s

        Yggdrasil

        Icelandic texts of the 13th century describe the great ash at the centre of the Norse cosmos, its roots reaching into the realms of the dead, an eagle in its crown and a serpent gnawing below.

      4. 1730

        Khejarli

        In Rajasthan, Amrita Devi and 362 other Bishnoi villagers were killed while embracing khejri trees to stop them being felled for a palace. Their story inspired later forest movements across India.

      5. 1945

        The survivor trees of Hiroshima

        About 170 trees within two kilometres of the atomic blast survived and grew again. The Japanese call them hibakujumoku. Their seeds have been sent to be planted around the world.

      6. 1973

        Chipko

        In the Himalayan foothills, village women held on to trees to stop commercial logging. Chipko means to cling. It became one of the best-known acts of non-violent environmental protest.

      7. 1977

        The Green Belt Movement

        Wangari Maathai began organising Kenyan women to plant trees for firewood, water and soil. The movement has since planted more than 51 million trees, and in 2004 she became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.

      8. 2001

        The Survivor Tree

        A Callery pear found burned and broken in the rubble of the World Trade Center was nursed back to health and returned to the memorial plaza in 2010. It flowers there each spring.

      Six layers, from bark to heart

      Only a thin sleeve of a mature trunk is alive. Choose a layer to see what it does.

        How a tree breathes and drinks

        A tree has no heart and no pump. Water rises because leaves lose it. As water evaporates through the stomata, it pulls on an unbroken column of water held together by its own cohesion, all the way down through the sapwood to the roots. In a tall conifer that column can be over 100 metres long.

        Leaves take in carbon dioxide by day. Every living cell, including those in the trunk and roots, also respires, taking in oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide around the clock. The pale flecks and slits on young bark are lenticels, openings that let the living tissue underneath exchange gases.

        Look behind this text. Pale streaks rise through the wood: water climbing from the roots. Amber flecks sink through the inner bark at the edges: sugar travelling down. The thin green line between them is the cambium, the only part of the trunk still growing.

        How a tree ages

        A tree grows from tips and from a sleeve. Buds lengthen shoots and roots; the cambium thickens the trunk. Each year it lays a new ring of wood over the last, so the oldest wood sits at the centre and the youngest just under the bark.

        Many ancient trees are hollow. Fungi slowly eat the dead heartwood while the living outer layers carry on. A hollow trunk can still be strong, and it becomes a home for bats, owls, beetles and fungi found nowhere else.

        The long death

        A dead tree is more alive than a living one.

        An old saying holds that an oak spends three hundred years growing, three hundred living and three hundred dying. That last stage feeds more life than the first two. Slide through what happens after a great tree begins to die.

        Wood-dwelling beetles alone make up a large share of forest life: about 1,340 of the roughly 8,000 beetle species in Central Europe, and around a fifth of all beetle species in Switzerland, live in or on wood. Half of Germany's wood-living beetles are on its red list of threatened species, because tidy forests remove the dead wood they need.

        Roots 0 to −3 m · below ground

        The dark half of the tree.

        Most of a tree's roots lie in the top 60 centimetres of soil, but they spread far, often two or three times as wide as the crown. At the tips, they are wrapped in fungi.

        A partnership 400 million years old

        Around nine in ten land plants live in partnership with mycorrhizal fungi. The fungus spreads threads called hyphae, a few thousandths of a millimetre wide and far finer than any root, through the soil. A single gram of forest soil can hold hundreds of metres of them.

        The fungus gathers water, nitrogen and phosphorus the roots could not reach. In return, the tree feeds it sugar: commonly estimated at up to a fifth of all the carbon it fixes.

        The "wood wide web"

        Because one fungus can colonise several trees, and one tree can host many fungi, roots of neighbouring trees can be linked through fungal threads. Ecologists call these common mycorrhizal networks.

        In 1997, Suzanne Simard and colleagues traced labelled carbon moving from paper birch to Douglas fir through the soil, and the journal Nature put the finding on its cover under the phrase "wood wide web". Later mapping in a Douglas fir forest found one large old tree linked to 47 others.

        The fungal kingdom

        The mushroom is only the fruit.

        The body of a fungus is the mycelium, a web of threads spread through soil and wood. A mushroom is just the part that pushes up to release spores. Fungi are why a forest floor is not buried under a mountain of dead wood.

        965 ha

        The humongous fungus

        In Oregon's Malheur National Forest, a single honey fungus, Armillaria ostoyae, spreads beneath about 965 hectares of forest. It is estimated to be at least 2,400 years old and possibly over 8,000. It is a parasite, slowly killing the conifers it lives on.

        Lignin

        The wood breakers

        Wood is made of cellulose glued with lignin, one of the toughest natural materials. White-rot fungi are among the very few organisms on Earth that can break lignin down completely. Without them, carbon locked in dead wood would barely return to the cycle.

        Scent

        Truffles and their animals

        Truffles fruit underground, where wind cannot spread their spores. Instead they release a strong smell when ripe. Squirrels, voles, wild boar and deer dig them up, eat them, and spread the spores in their droppings, often near the roots of new host trees.

        Lichen

        Two lives in one

        A lichen is a fungus living with an alga or a cyanobacterium, which makes food from light. Together they grow on bare bark where neither could live alone. Some, like lungwort, take decades to establish and only grow on old trees in clean air.

        Mother trees

        Simard uses the term mother tree for the large, old hub trees in these networks. Their deep roots and wide fungal connections may help young trees nearby establish, especially in shade or drought. Some greenhouse experiments found Douglas fir seedlings sent slightly more carbon to their own kin than to strangers.

        The idea is moving and has changed how some forests are managed, with old trees left standing when others are cut. It is also one of the most debated ideas in forest ecology, because it is hard to show that a tree, rather than its fungus, is directing the flow, or that the amounts move enough to matter.

        The soil itself is alive

        A teaspoon of healthy forest soil can hold more microorganisms than there are people on Earth: bacteria, fungi, protists, tiny nematodes. Springtails and mites graze the fungal threads. Earthworms pull fallen leaves down into the dark.

        Together they turn last autumn's leaves into next spring's nutrients. Compacting soil, by trampling, parking or heavy machines, crushes the pore spaces this life depends on. Protecting the ground under a tree's crown is one of the kindest things you can do for it.

        Forest, river and sea

        Some forests are made of salmon.

        Along the rivers of Alaska and British Columbia, salmon swim in from the ocean to spawn and die. Bears catch them and carry them into the trees. What the bears leave behind feeds the forest.

        On Chichagof Island in southeast Alaska, researchers traced nitrogen with a chemical fingerprint found only in the ocean. Sitka spruce growing beside salmon streams drew about a quarter of the nitrogen in their needles from salmon. Near spawning sites, the spruce grew more than three times as fast as spruce beside streams without salmon.

        The trees return the favour. Their shade keeps the water cool enough for young salmon. Insects fall from the branches into the stream as food. Fallen logs make the pools and hiding places that young fish need.

        The forest is the lover of the sea

        In the late 1980s, Shigeatsu Hatakeyama, an oyster farmer in Kesennuma Bay in Japan, watched his oysters fail. He came to believe the sea's health depended on the forests upstream, which filter the rain and send nutrients down the rivers to the bay. So he and other fishers began planting trees in the mountains.

        Their movement is called Mori wa Umi no Koibito: the forest is the lover of the sea.

        Being honest about the evidence

        What we know, and what is still argued

        In 2023, Justine Karst and colleagues reviewed the field research and warned that popular accounts had run ahead of the evidence. Here is how the claims stand.

        Well supported

        Established

        • Most trees depend on mycorrhizal fungi for water and nutrients.
        • Fungal networks can physically link neighbouring trees.
        • Labelled carbon and nitrogen can move between trees through soil.
        • Trees release airborne chemicals when attacked, which neighbours can respond to.
        Shown in some experiments

        Promising

        • Defence-related signals passing between seedlings through fungal links, seen in greenhouse pots with fir and pine, and with bean plants.
        • Seedlings joined to a network sometimes survive better, though other studies find no benefit.
        • Hub trees with many links.
        Debated

        Contested

        • Whether network transfers are large enough to matter in mature forests.
        • Whether trees deliberately send help, or fungi move resources for their own benefit.
        • That forests are mainly cooperative. Trees also compete hard for light and water.
        • Words like "talk", "share" and "mother", which can be helpful images or can mislead.
